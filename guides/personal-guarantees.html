Personal guarantees: what directors need to know

A limited company protects you, until you sign a personal guarantee. Here’s how they work, and what to do if one gets called in.

What a personal guarantee is

A personal guarantee is your promise to pay a company debt if the company can’t. It sits outside the protection a limited company normally gives you, so the lender can come after you personally, and in the worst case, your home.

If more than one director signed, the guarantee is usually “joint and several”. That means the lender can chase any one of you for the full amount, not just your share.

Where they turn up

  • Bank and business loansMost lenders ask for one, especially on unsecured loans.
  • Merchant cash advancesMany include a guarantee. Check exactly what yours covers.
  • Asset financeCommon for newer companies and bigger agreements.
  • Commercial leasesLandlords often ask directors to guarantee the rent.
  • Supplier accountsEasy to miss. Some credit account forms have a guarantee in the small print.

Covid loans are different

Bounce Back Loans didn’t need a personal guarantee. CBILS loans under £250,000 couldn’t have one either, and on bigger CBILS loans your main home couldn’t be used as security.

That doesn’t mean there’s no personal risk. If a Bounce Back Loan was misused or the application wasn’t honest, directors can still be held personally responsible (see other ways you can be liable).

When a guarantee gets called in

Lenders usually call in a guarantee after the company defaults or goes into insolvency. You’ll get a formal demand. If it isn’t paid, the lender can take you to court and, for debts of £5,000 or more, apply to make you bankrupt.

This is why keeping the company trading matters. If the company keeps paying under an agreed plan, the guarantee usually never gets called in.

Check your guarantee

  • Get a copy of exactly what you signed
  • Is it capped at a set amount, or unlimited?
  • Is it “all monies”, covering every debt the company owes that lender?
  • Did you sign alone, or jointly with other directors?
  • Is it secured on your home or other property?
  • Have the loan terms changed since you signed, without your agreement? That can sometimes affect the guarantee, so get legal advice.

If yours has been called in

  1. Don’t ignore it. Deadlines matter, and silence makes court action more likely.
  2. Get the paperwork. The signed guarantee, the loan agreement and the demand.
  3. Get independent legal advice on whether the guarantee can be enforced, and for how much.
  4. Talk to the lender. Many will agree a payment plan or a settlement based on what you can realistically pay.

Other ways you can be liable

Overdrawn director’s loan account
If the company goes into liquidation, the liquidator can ask you to pay it back.
Wrongful trading
Carrying on when you knew, or should have known, the company couldn’t avoid insolvency, without doing all you could to protect creditors.
Misconduct
Courts can order disqualified directors to compensate creditors personally, for example over a misused Bounce Back Loan. The Insolvency Service can also investigate directors of dissolved companies and disqualify them for up to 15 years.
Some unpaid taxes
In certain cases HMRC can make directors personally liable, such as unpaid National Insurance caused by fraud or neglect.

Before you sign another one

  • Ask for a cap on how much you can be liable for
  • Keep your home out of it if you can
  • Ask for the guarantee to fall away once the balance drops below a set level
  • Get independent legal advice before you sign
  • Look at personal guarantee insurance, which can cover part of what you’d owe. It usually has a waiting period, so it’s for new guarantees, not ones already in trouble.
